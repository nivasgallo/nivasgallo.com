The problem.

Betfair's Sportsbook and Exchange were separate legacy apps, with little content management and almost no room to experiment. Design changes were slow to ship and hard to test with customers.

Rebuild replaced the Sportsbook app with The Betfair Destination, TBD: a single web and native platform that gave design one experience to improve. It was first meant to bring the Sportsbook and Exchange together in one product.

Structure first.

Before any screens, the new product needed a structure that worked for every sport. Global features, like the account, My Bets and search, sit beside the sports, and sports are grouped by how customers actually find a bet:

  • Competition-driven, like football, cricket and tennis: sport, competition, event, market.
  • Meeting-driven, like horse and greyhound racing: sport, meeting, market.
  • Event-driven sports, which skip the competition level.
  • Virtual sports, on their own.

Navigation stayed flat: four tabs, Home, Browse, My Bets and Casino, the pattern Apple recommends for apps with several parallel journeys. Every journey starts one tap from the bottom bar, with fewer layers to reach content.

Breaking the industry pattern.

For Bet Builder, our Same Game Multi, the industry-standard flow had customers pick markets, then step through to odds, then open the bet slip. We assumed customers wanted that too.

Testing it against a simpler model showed the opposite: tap the odds you want, straight from the market, and build the bet in the bet slip, like any other multiple. Each new selection collapses the bet slip into a tip that shows the bet growing, such as "Build a £10 bet for returns of £360.00".

We chose the simpler route, even though it meant more engineering work, because it took friction out of placing a bet and made it much faster.

One product, or two?

Rebuild was meant to bring the Sportsbook and Exchange together. That raised a question customers shouldn't have to think about: which product am I in? Most people want the one they came for, as fast as possible. Switching is a second step.

I explored six ways to let customers set their product preference, and weighed each against how often people would really switch:

  • A settings toggle. The most expected place to set content preferences.
  • A content module. A visual prompt tied to a customer goal, such as placing multiples.
  • Onboarding. Choose at first login, and learn where to change it later.
  • Smart detection. Offer to switch when someone keeps toggling to the other product.
  • A hidden gesture. Not recommended: hard to discover.
  • An always-visible switch. Not recommended: another decision on every page, with no data to show people need it.

The saved default-product setting outlived Rebuild. It's still part of onboarding in Phoenix.

Bet without losing your place.

On the Exchange, prices and liquidity move constantly, and a bet slip that covers the market hides exactly what customers need to see. The Exchange bet slip opens inline, attached to the price button, so the rest of the market stays in view while you bet.

In research with customers in November 2020, people found it:

  • Showed odds movement, liquidity and the rest of the market more clearly
  • Made it clearer which selection they were betting on
  • Made it easier to edit an unmatched bet
  • Felt faster to place a bet
  • Made unmatched bets by mistake less likely

The inline bet slip outlived Rebuild too: it's the one Phoenix kept.

Fix what research finds.

Research in December 2020 showed search worked: it was easy to find and use, searching for a team worked well, and one participant found the markets they wanted by typing "BTTS".

But the keyboard covered the results while people typed, and a search for "Today's racing" returned nothing. The proposed fix kept results visible above the keyboard, grouped by sport and competition.

Two kinds of prototype, two jobs.

I coached the team to separate them. Dynamic prototypes went in front of customers, often several interaction options side by side, and we kept the ones that scored best for ease of use, accessibility and conversion.

Static prototypes became the documentation: annotated hand-off notes and specs for engineers and the design system team. Every state was drawn out, down to a closed market, a changed price or a stake over the limit, so nothing was left for engineers to guess.

Designs that explain themselves.

Our files were hard to navigate, and every design needed a designer in the room to explain it. I helped rebuild how we worked: clearer file organisation, standardised assets and shared ceremonies, so stakeholders could understand a design without a walkthrough, and user tests ran on something closer to the real product.

TBD's bold, colourful visual language came out of the same spirit. It was built collaboratively, through design workshops across the team.
